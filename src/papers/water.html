The Leeds Water Guarantee

A proposal to guarantee every household in Leeds enough water for a decent life, starting with those who can least afford it.

Summary

  • The commitment. Everyone in Leeds has access to enough safe water for drinking, cooking, washing and sanitation, regardless of income.
  • What a Leeds household pays for water is set by a statutory licence, a regulator’s five-year settlement and a company tariff. Law and policy made all three, and law and policy can change them.{{cite:cl-own,yw-scheme}}
  • The average Yorkshire Water household bill is £636 in 2026/27.{{cite:wateruk}} If the national water-poverty rate of 6.8% applies in Leeds, about {{n:city.poverty}} of the city’s 341,466 households spend more than 5% of their income after housing costs on water.{{cite:ccw-poverty,census}} No Leeds figure exists.
  • We propose an Essential Water Allowance of 80 litres per person per day, within the United Nations’ guide of 50 to 100 litres,{{cite:un}} credited at no cost, with standing charges also covered.
  • For households in water poverty the allowance would cost about {{n:a.total}} a year at 2026/27 prices, which is {{n:a.shareOfBills}} of the {{n:city.allBills}} that Leeds households pay for water each year. It is worth {{n:a.perHousehold}} a year to an average-sized household, more than the typical water-poverty gap of £337.{{cite:ccw-poverty}}
  • A universal allowance cannot be paid for by higher users alone. At 80 litres it would raise the price of every litre above the allowance by {{n:u80.surchargePct}}. The cost has to be shared between bill payers, the company and its investors, and government.
  • The first steps are a 100-home, 12-month pilot costing £87,960, and putting the allowance in the priorities of the new Yorkshire regional water council. Local leaders’ proposals for those councils are due by the end of 2026.{{cite:defra-nov}}

Decisions sought

Five decisions, each addressed to the body that can take it

  1. Mayor of West YorkshireName an Essential Water Allowance as a priority in West Yorkshire’s proposal for the Yorkshire regional water council, due by the end of 2026.
  2. Secretary of State for EnvironmentInclude a guaranteed essential volume of water among the affordability objectives in the Government’s direction to the next price review.
  3. HM GovernmentUse the Water Bill and the 10-year plan to give mayors powers over affordability as well as performance, and commit that the returns from any public stake pay for essential water first.
  4. Yorkshire Water and a housing partnerAgree a 100-home, 12-month pilot that credits current charges at source, with independent evaluation and a resident panel. Indicative cost £87,960.
  5. Leeds City CouncilCommission the missing evidence: a Leeds measure of water poverty, the share of homes with a meter, and the number of renters who pay for water through rent or service charges.

1How a company comes to charge for water

1.1

Yorkshire Water does not own a right to charge for water. It holds an instrument of appointment, the statutory licence created when the regional water authorities were privatised in 1989. The appointment makes it the water and sewerage undertaker for its area and gives it a duty to supply.{{cite:cl-own}}

1.2

Every five years the regulator decides how much revenue the company may collect. The company then divides that revenue between customers in an annual charges scheme. In 2026/27 a metered household in Leeds pays £2.1838 for each cubic metre of water and £2.9761 for each cubic metre of sewerage, charged on 95% of the water supplied, plus standing charges of £54.10 for water and £33.85 for sewerage.{{cite:yw-meter,yw-scheme}}

1.3

The price of a glass of water in Leeds therefore comes from three things: a licence, a regulator’s settlement and a tariff. Law and policy made all three, and law and policy can change them. Since 1999 a household cannot be cut off for not paying, but it can still fall into debt.{{cite:ccw-debt}}

1.4

In 2010 the United Nations recognised water and sanitation as a human right, and it gives a guide of 50 to 100 litres per person per day for basic needs.{{cite:un}} England has no legal entitlement to any volume of water at any price. Average household use in England is 136.9 litres per person per day.{{cite:ea}}

1.5

This points to two separate questions. Access is about whether every household can afford the water it needs. Ownership is about who holds the pipes and takes the returns. Access can be guaranteed now, through the tariff and the next price review. Ownership will take longer, because it goes through Parliament. This paper deals with access first and sets out a route to ownership in Part 6.

2The commitment, defined

2.1

Enough. We propose 80 litres per person per day: the middle of the UN range and about three-fifths of average household use in England. The allowance covers essential use, not all use. Water used above it is charged as now.

2.2

Safe. The quality of drinking water is already regulated. This paper is about who can afford water, not about its quality.

2.3

Regardless of income. No household should go without essential water because of cost. The guarantee would start with households in water poverty and widen as funding and evidence allow.

2.4

How it reaches a household. A metered home receives a credit for the allowance volume at the published price. A home without a meter receives a deemed credit of the same value. Eligible households also have their standing charges covered. The credit appears on the normal bill, so nobody has to pay first and claim the money back.

2.5

Existing help stays. About 2.3 million households in England and Wales are already on water social tariffs,{{cite:tariffs}} and Yorkshire Water puts £2.5m a year of its own money into its schemes.{{cite:yw-assure}} The allowance would sit on top of these schemes, funding only the balance still due.

3What it would cost

3.1

The cost is the allowance in litres, multiplied by 365 days and by the combined price of {{n:city.rate}} per cubic metre supplied, for each person covered. Standing charges are added where they are covered, plus 3% for running and evaluation. Annex A sets out the method, and every assumption can be changed in the model.

The recommended scenario. Covering the estimated {{n:a.households}} households in water poverty costs {{n:a.total}} a year. If bills rise by 5% a year, that becomes {{n:a@2030.total}} in 2030/31 and {{n:a@2035.total}} in 2035/36, a ten-year total of {{n:a.tenyear}}.

3.3

The universal case. Covering every household at 80 litres, with standing charges, costs {{n:d.total}} a year. Paid for entirely by higher users, it would add {{n:u80.surcharge}} to every cubic metre used above the allowance, a {{n:u80.surchargePct}} increase. That would move the cost onto large families and onto people who need more water for medical reasons, so we do not recommend it. Scenario C shows that a universal minimum of 50 litres can be paid for through the tariff, but only by raising the price above the allowance by {{n:c.surchargePct}}.

3.4

Limits of the estimate. The Leeds figures apply national rates of water poverty, and the £337 gap dates from 2024/25. The model does not adjust for households using less water when its price rises. These limits are listed in Part 10.

6.1

Kelda Holdings, which owns Yorkshire Water, is owned 42% by EQT, 42% by GIC and 16% by TCorp, the New South Wales public sector investment manager.{{cite:eqt,yp}} EQT’s purchase was reported to value Kelda at about £9.4 billion, roughly 90% of its regulatory capital value.{{cite:pew}} Once the caps are lifted, the Government could take a stake. If it does, the first call on that stake’s returns should be the allowance.

6.2

Leeds has owned its water before. In 1852 Leeds Corporation bought the waterworks company and went on to supply the city from Eccup.{{cite:leeds-1852}} New housing sites can already be served by a new appointee.{{cite:ofwat-nav}} But local ownership does not by itself make water affordable. A locally owned company still has to recover its costs. The access guarantee is needed whoever owns the pipes.

7Precedents

7.1

South Africa. National policy since 2001 has provided 6,000 litres of free water per household per month, with charges above that.{{cite:sa-fbw}} It shows that a free basic allowance can work at national scale. It also shows the weakness of a flat household figure: large households receive less per person.

7.2

Ireland. Household water is paid for through general taxation. The national utility uses an allowance of 213,000 litres a year per household as the threshold for its conservation charge.{{cite:uisce}} An allowance can be generous and still keep a signal against waste.

7.3

Leeds. The city’s own history is the third precedent. A council that once bought its waterworks can at least ask for a guarantee of essential water.{{cite:leeds-1852}}

8Objections and risks

8.1

“Other bill payers will pay for it.” In Scenario A the tariff element adds {{n:a.surchargePct}} to the price of water used above the allowance. We reject a universal allowance paid for only through the tariff, for the reasons in paragraph 3.3.

8.2

“Free water encourages waste.” The allowance is about three-fifths of average use, and water above it is charged as now or slightly more, so the incentive to save water remains for most use. In a dry year that matters.

8.3

“Targeting will miss people.” It will, unless households are enrolled automatically. The main risk in Scenario A is take-up. With consent, data already held for benefits could identify eligible households without a new application.

8.4

“This is not Leeds’s decision.” The tariff is not, which is why the decisions sought go to the bodies that hold each power. What Leeds can do is collect the evidence and test the idea on a small scale before it is taken further.

8.5

“Renters and unmetered homes.” Where water is in the rent or a service charge, the credit must be shown to reach the resident. Unmetered homes need a deemed credit. Both are open questions, listed in Part 10.

8.6

“A national social tariff would be simpler.” It might be. The Government decided against one in January 2026.{{cite:mse}} A working local guarantee would show what a national one could look like.

9The first step: a 100-home pilot

9.1

For twelve months, pay the current water and sewerage charges of 100 households in one area, working with a housing partner. For the pilot we cover the whole current bill, not just the allowance volume, because that is simpler to run and to measure. Existing discounts stay in place, and the pilot pays only the balance still due.

9.2

Conditions. Funding for credits is released only after real bills are costed, the payment agreement with Yorkshire Water is signed and a full year of cover is secured. Historic arrears are dealt with separately. No partner or funder has yet committed.

9.3

What a resident sees. A usual bill showing the charges, a matching credit and nothing left to pay. Residents can join on paper, by phone or in person, and joining never requires a smart meter.

9.4

What is measured. Arrears and debt, how households use water, wellbeing, the cost of running the pilot per home, and how many invited households take part. The results are published in full, including any that count against the proposal.

10What we do not yet know

10.1

How much water poverty there is in Leeds. We apply the England and Wales rate. A Leeds estimate could use the same method as the Consumer Council for Water’s study with local data on incomes and housing costs.{{cite:ccw-poverty}}

10.2

How many homes have a meter. Yorkshire Water holds this figure. It decides how much of the allowance can be credited by volume and how much by deemed credit.

10.3

How many renters pay for water through rent. Housing providers and the council hold parts of this picture.

10.4

How large water-poor households are, and how use responds to price. Both affect the cost. The pilot would provide the first local evidence on each.

AMethod

A.1

Combined price per cubic metre supplied = water price + 0.95 × sewerage price = {{n:city.rate}} at 2026/27 prices.{{cite:yw-meter,yw-scheme}}

A.2

Value per person per year = allowance (litres) × 365 ÷ 1,000 × combined price. At 80 litres this is {{n:a.m3}} m³, worth {{n:a.perPerson}}.

A.3

Households covered = 341,466 × the share eligible (6.8%, 15% or 100%). People covered = households × {{n:city.hhSize}}, the average household size from Census 2021.{{cite:census}}

A.4

Annual cost = (people covered × value per person + households covered × standing charges, if covered) × 1.03 for running and evaluation.

A.5

Tariff element = the cost share given to higher users ÷ (all Leeds residents × the average annual use above the allowance), using average use of 136.9 litres per person per day.{{cite:ea}}

A.6

Future years apply the chosen bill growth rate, 5% a year by default, to prices and standing charges. The model and these calculations share one code file, and a check script confirms that every figure in this paper matches the model.
